Section 01

The Financial Case

The business argument for walkable, mixed-use development is no longer primarily philosophical. The data is available, it comes from multiple independent sources, and it holds up under stress conditions that conventional development did not survive.

Walkable neighborhoods hold value through downturns
D1·S1 · Hedonic pricing research across 15 years

A Louisville new urbanist community that started in 2000, Norton Commons has now been tracked long enough to show what walkable design does to price over time. From $135/sqft at launch to $234 by 2019 — the premium relative to surrounding comparables grew steadily, not in a single event. During COVID, when car-dependent suburban living became an obvious liability, Norton Commons held 17% above its peer group.

The researcher's term for what buyers are actually purchasing: community membership. Standard attributes — bedrooms, square footage, lot size — showed almost no correlation with sale price variation. Which community the home was in explained the premium. The implication for LP is that marketing individual home features is probably less effective than establishing what Trillium membership means.

The research also found an 80-100 acre threshold below which the walkable community effect doesn't materialize. Projects smaller than that don't have enough critical mass to feel like a place. Trillium clears this threshold comfortably.

The neighborhood a child grows up in shapes adult earnings more than almost any other single factor
D2·CK · Harvard economist, closing keynote

Raj Chetty's team analyzed a massive natural experiment: Hope VI, which replaced severely distressed public housing with mixed-income walkable neighborhoods. Children who moved into the revitalized neighborhoods as kids earned 20% more as adults than comparable children who stayed in conventional housing. The effect scaled with years of exposure and applied only to children — adults who moved in showed essentially no earnings benefit.

The mechanism Chetty identified as the strongest predictor of economic mobility: cross-class friendship. The fraction of high-income friends that low-income people have, measured through Facebook connections across 72 million users, is more predictive than school quality, poverty rate, or any single policy intervention. Walkable mixed-use communities produce these connections structurally, through daily proximity at the coffee shop, the market, the park.

The government ROI calculation: revitalizing one unit costs about $170K. Higher future earnings produce roughly $100K in tax revenue. Net cost per unit: $70K, with a $25K/year per-unit benefit in perpetuity. Chetty's summary for city finance directors: "This is cheaper than you think, and it pays back."

One important caveat he named: the effect is highly context-dependent. Moving into a walkable neighborhood surrounded by low-opportunity neighborhoods shows minimal benefit. The place has to actually connect to something worth connecting to. For Trillium, that means the regional connectivity — trails, roads, proximity to employment — matters to the case LP makes about long-term value.

NWA's 62% one-and-two-person household rate makes Trillium's product mix a market decision, not a design preference
D1·S4 · Attainability session data

NWA has 62% one-and-two-person households — more concentrated than the national average of roughly 55-57%. That's not a social trend to accommodate. It's the primary market. A development that leads with four-bedroom single-family homes as its main product is designed for the 38%, not the 62%.

Trillium's product mix — smaller homes, cottage lots, missing middle housing — is the mathematically correct response to who lives in NWA. This argument is more persuasive to investors and more honest in entitlement conversations than framing it as a design preference or a social mission. The market exists. The product is undersupplied.

A note on methodology: the 62% figure comes from NWA census data, and there is internal LP disagreement about whether it's the right baseline for Cave Springs specifically. Pull the census tract-level data for Cave Springs before using this number in investor or entitlement conversations. The figure is likely directionally correct; the precision matters for specific arguments.

Deal Structure

The financial outcomes of great places come not just from design but from how the deal is put together. The practitioners who survived market downturns structured their projects so they weren't holding debt when things went bad.

No bank debt is a strategic position, not just a financing preference
D1·S2 · David Johnston, developer

Norton Commons raised equity, not debt. When the 2008 crash came and neighboring developers were underwater on construction loans, Norton Commons had no bank debt to service. Johnston's description: "giants among pygmies." The equity-first model required patient capital and required convincing investors to accept slower returns — but it also meant the project didn't get force-sold or design-compromised during a downturn.

The builders' guild structure: Norton Commons never sold lots to individual homeowners. Lots went only to vetted builders, released in batches of 25-30 to maintain scarcity and prevent vacant "missing teeth" in the street wall. All sales ran through one in-house office telling one consistent story. This kind of control over sequence, pace, and quality is structural — not something you enforce after the fact.

The asset retention argument Johnston made: every time you build to sell, you pay tax and exit the appreciation. Norton Commons retained commercial and rental assets, which have appreciated alongside the residential development. The retained assets now generate income while LP continues to develop. Worth deciding early which Trillium assets to sell and which to hold.

The project went from an $8.7 million land purchase to a development worth over $1.5 billion. Johnston's comment on the timeline: "This didn't happen in five years."

Investors who need to exit in 7 years will eventually compromise a 25-year vision
D3·S4 · Lessons of Failure panel

Institutional fund structures have 10-year lives, which means individual projects must exit in 7 years — sometimes 3-5 — to return capital on schedule. New urban development that takes 20-30 years to reach its full expression is structurally incompatible with this model.

The consequences show up in predictable ways: projects designed to exit rather than mature; communities whose developer-champions leave before the place reaches its expression; second-generation buyers who came for appreciation rather than community. The panel called this one of the movement's persistent structural failures.

LP should examine whether any current or prospective investor partners are operating on a fund timeline. If so, the resolution needs to happen before it becomes a crisis during a downturn, not after.

You can't out-build production builders — but you can out-craft them
D1·S9 · Building Better panel; D3·S4 · Lessons of Failure

You cannot compete with production builders on economies of scale — they have more units, lower per-unit cost, and that's just math. The alternative is economy of means: building with lower capital inputs, simpler construction, dimensions dictated by available materials rather than by architectural ambition. The small traditional house is the size it is because that's what you could buy at the lumber store.

Seaside is more economically vital than Celebration, despite Celebration's much larger scale, partly because Seaside was built with very loose guidelines, very low investment threshold per building, and many independent developers making individual decisions. That produced variety, vitality, and ferment. Celebration's tightly controlled, Disney-funded approach produced more architectural consistency and less life.

The practitioner reference LP should visit: New Town St. Charles (St. Charles, Missouri), where town architect Greg Whitaker has solved the economy-of-means problem at production scale. Simple, repeatable construction that still achieves the urban behaviors Trillium's code will require. See it before finalizing builder standards.

Retail Science

Bob Fields (retail planning consultant) presented the most data-dense session of the conference. These are not principles — they're working metrics.

+30%

The first question Fields asks every client: What is your exit strategy for the retail? Profit center (to sell to an institutional fund), amenity (to increase housing values), or legacy (to do good for the community)? These lead to completely different designs, financing structures, and management expectations. LP hasn't explicitly answered this for Trillium's commercial areas.

The pedestrian shed rule: A retail district can support roughly $1M in annual retail sales per acre within a quarter-mile pedestrian shed — the area people will walk to without thinking about it. Beyond a quarter mile, foot traffic falls off sharply. Design implications: concentrate retail within a tight pedestrian shed rather than spreading it across a large area.

Retail sales multipliers: A walkable green grocer adds approximately 30% to surrounding retail sales. A 30,000 sqft grocery anchor adds about 25%. Street trees add 15%. A library nearby adds 5%. Festivals, counterintuitively, reduce retail sales on the days they occur — they consume parking and draw a different population.

On free parking: Free parking does not increase retail sales — it subsidizes the car and transfers the cost to someone else. On-street parking that turns 4 times a day generates $175K in annual retail sales per space. Fields cited Shoup's parking economics directly: eliminate minimums, price at market, return revenue to the street.

The Seabrook model: A lease-back structure where the developer leases retail space at below-market rates to a curated anchor tenant, guaranteeing them a viable business while maintaining merchandising control. The arrangement converts what would be a profit-center tenant into a community anchor that makes the surrounding retail more viable. Worth understanding before Trillium's first commercial lease is negotiated.

IRC vs. IBC cost cliff: 3-story walk-up buildings under the International Residential Code run approximately $230/sqft to build in Atlanta (2024 data). 4-story buildings crossing the threshold into International Building Code requirements jump to around $300/sqft. 5-story podium construction: $450+/sqft. The cliff between 3 and 4 stories is not linear — it's a wall. This has direct implications for what LP can financially justify in the village center.

Early retail activation doesn't require 500 rooftops — it requires 300 square feet and one committed operator
D1·S5 · Steve Mouzon, Original Green; originalgreen.org/resources/original-green-scale/single-crew-workplaces/

Steve Mouzon's concept addresses the "you need 500 rooftops before you can support retail" problem that kills early activation in new developments. A single-crew workplace is a business small enough to be run by one person or one small team: a grocer needs 400 sqft, a restaurant needs 300 sqft, a barber needs 200 sqft.

These businesses can operate from day one with very few customers. They don't need a fully built-out neighborhood to be viable. They also contribute more to walkability and street character than the large-format retail they eventually make way for. Trillium's pre-population activation strategy should start here — pop-up and single-crew businesses before the houses fill in, not after.

Construction Cost Structure

The jump from 3 to 4 stories is not incremental — it changes the building type and nearly doubles the cost
D1·S4 · Attainability session; D3·S1 · Single-stair buildings

The IRC/IBC transition is the single most consequential building code decision for Trillium's cost structure. Under IRC (International Residential Code), buildings of up to 3 stories can be built with stick-frame wood construction, simpler fire protection requirements, and no commercial building code overhead. The moment you add a fourth story, the project falls under IBC (International Building Code), which requires sprinkler systems, more complex egress arrangements, and fire-rated construction that often requires masonry or heavier structural systems.

Atlanta 2024 construction cost benchmarks by building type:

  • Duplexes (IRC)~$160/sf
  • 3-story walk-up (IRC)~$230/sf
  • 4-story surface-parked (IBC)~$300/sf
  • 5-story podium (IBC)~$450+/sf

Vertical mixed-use (retail under residential) adds roughly 25% to construction cost but rarely produces 25% more rent. Separate retail buildings on the ground with residential above — accessed as distinct buildings — avoid much of the code complexity and cost premium while achieving the same street character. The IRC allows buildings with commercial ground-floor use if the floor area and occupancy stay within residential code limits.

The single-stair building specifically offers a way to build multifamily at 3 stories with significantly better unit quality (corner exposure, daylight on multiple sides) and lower cost (no double-loaded corridor, more leasable area per sqft of building) than a conventional apartment building. See Section 2 for the fire safety data.

Practitioner Wisdom on Development Strategy

Start Small. Execute Perfectly.
D1·S9 · Building Better panel; D2·S3 · Jeff Speck

The most consistent advice from the most experienced developers in the room: whatever land you have, pretend you only have a fraction of it for the first phase. Norton Commons released lots in batches of 25-30 — always maintaining scarcity — and developed neighborhood by neighborhood rather than spreading across the whole site. The Olmsted neighborhoods in Louisville, which Norton Commons used as a precedent, were built block by block by different landowners over decades and read today as if they were planned all at once.

Jeff Speck's LoDo principle from Denver: don't sprinkle walkability across the whole development. Pick the smallest possible spot, meet all the requirements for a great walkable place in that spot, and let the energy radiate outward. Denver's Lower Downtown was one block — a brewery, a train station, some good sidewalks and facades. That one block became a neighborhood. For Trillium: which specific block or street becomes the one that's done right first?

The "is it meaningful?" test: every developer on the Building Better panel eventually traced their resilience through hard development cycles back to whether the project meant something to them. "Is this project going to make a difference? Or is it just a project?" The projects that got sold or compromised during 2008 were mostly the ones where the developer's commitment was financial rather than vocational.

The public health case for walkable communities now has institutional backing
D1·S9 · Building Better panel

The former US Surgeon General released an advisory in 2023 consolidating research on the relationship between built environment and health outcomes. The conclusion: living in communities built on new urbanist principles is associated with better health, greater happiness, and longer life expectancy. The conference community spent years making this argument without data. The data now exists, from an institutional source with public health credibility that goes beyond real estate research.

This belongs in LP's investor deck alongside Chetty's economic mobility data. It gives three independent institutional sources — financial (Norton Commons), economic (Harvard), and public health (Surgeon General) — all pointing to the same conclusion. The argument is no longer philosophical.
