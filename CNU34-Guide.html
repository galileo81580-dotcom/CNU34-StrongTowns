The Short Version

Ten Things Worth Knowing

If you read nothing else, read this.

1
The financial case for walkable urbanism is now empirical. Norton Commons prices rose from $135/sqft in 2005 to $234 in 2019 and held 17% above comparables during COVID. Raj Chetty's Harvard research shows children who grow up in walkable new urban neighborhoods earn 20% more as adults. The philosophical argument is over. The data argument is available.
2
Deal structure is as important as design. Norton Commons survived 2008 without bank debt — "giants among pygmies." Equity over leverage, land-bought-as-sold, builders carrying their own financing: these structural choices matter as much as the site plan.
3
Phase 1 is the whole argument. The test: if Phase 1 was all that ever got built, would it feel like a place? A complete slice through all product types — not the easiest products in the easiest corner. Phase 1 isn't a development phase. It's the proof of concept.
4
Every successful village center started one-story. Rosemary Beach demolished its last one-story building two years ago. Andres Duany called multi-story mixed-use masonry in Phase 1 "trying to give birth to a teenager." Design the infant. Plan for the adult.
5
The town architect has to be in place before the first builder arrives. Not after. The Lakeland comparison is specific: same quality master plan as Kentlands, no town architect, dramatically inferior result twenty years later. Vinyl everything. Enclosure that doesn't enclose.
6
Good design is necessary but not sufficient. Third Street Cottages had the right physical patterns and still needed a governance rebuild when buyers who came for the aesthetic got on the HOA board. The community agreements, governance structure, and founding vision document require as much intentionality as the hardware.
7
New apartments are 6× safer than new single-family homes. Single-stair buildings in NYC and Seattle have had zero egress-related fire deaths across 12 years of study. The 20-foot street rule dates to 1915 fire underwriters calculating clearance for two horse-drawn carriages. It's a convention, not a safety finding.
8
NWA's 62% one-and-two-person household rate validates Trillium's product mix. It's basic market math, not a social mission. The demand for smaller homes, cottage lots, and missing middle product is higher here than in most comparable markets.
9
The planning/fire relationship can be a design asset. Fayetteville's city planner and fire planning chief built a productive working relationship in two years — monthly meetings, joint city council advocacy, no crisis required. When fire officials show up at city council supporting a rezoning, the political dynamic changes completely. Cave Springs is 20 minutes away.
10
The risk of default to conventional development in NWA is real. The regional data shows 41% of NWA city land is still undeveloped. The choices made about that land in the next decade will compound for a century. Trillium Village is the most visible private-sector counterexample to the conventional path.
About This Guide

Source Material

Synthesized from 26 recorded sessions at CNU 34 (Bentonville/Fayetteville, May 12–15, 2026). Recordings are in the LP Otter.ai account. Individual session notes — drawn directly from transcripts — are in the Session Archive.

The guide is organized thematically rather than by day because the same ideas surfaced across multiple sessions, and the connections between them are more useful than the sequence. Where a session is the primary source for a claim, it's cited by session code (e.g., D1·S2 = Day 1, Session 2).

A few speakers appear frequently enough that first-name references are used: David Johnston (Norton Commons, Louisville); Bob Fields (retail planning); Jeff Speck (urban designer, Walkable City); Eric Klinenberg (NYU, Palaces for the People); Andres Duany (DPZ, CNU co-founder). Full attributions in session notes.
